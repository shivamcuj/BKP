Electron Transport Chain

"Energy flowing through the heart of the cell."

Detailed Explanations

1. Introduction to the ETC

The Electron Transport Chain (ETC), affectionately also known as the Respiratory Chain, is the grand finale of cellular respiration. The final steps in the overall oxidation of foodstuffs (carbohydrates, fats, and amino acids) result in the formation of energy-rich coenzymes: NADH and FADH2.

The ETC oxidizes these coenzymes by transferring their electrons (reducing equivalents) through a series of oxidation-reduction reactions. The terminal electron acceptor in this journey is Oxygen (O2). In the presence of oxygen, the ETC beautifully converts these reducing equivalents into usable cellular energy—ATP—via a process called oxidative phosphorylation.

Localization

Where does this magic happen? The electron transport chain is nestled snugly within the inner mitochondrial membrane. The enzymes and complexes that orchestrate this electron dance are intimately embedded within this membrane, acting as a bridge between the mitochondrial matrix and the cytosolic side.

2. Components & Complexes

The ETC is composed of several key players, most of which are proteins (with one notable lipid-soluble exception). They are structurally organized into four major complexes:

  • I
    Complex I (NADH-CoQ Reductase): Catalyzes the transfer of electrons from NADH to Coenzyme Q (CoQ). It utilizes Flavin mononucleotide (FMN) and Iron-Sulfur (Fe-S) proteins.
  • II
    Complex II (Succinate-CoQ Reductase): Transfers electrons directly from Succinate (via FADH2) to Coenzyme Q, bypassing Complex I.
  • III
    Complex III (CoQ-Cytochrome c Reductase): Transfers electrons from Coenzyme Q to Cytochrome c. It contains cytochromes b and c1, and Fe-S proteins.
  • IV
    Complex IV (Cytochrome Oxidase): The final stage. It transfers electrons from Cytochrome c to Oxygen (O2), forming water. It contains cytochromes a and a3 (which contain copper).

Note on Cytochromes: Cytochrome c is water-soluble and easily diffuses. Cytochromes b, c1, a, and a3 are lipid-soluble and fixed in the membrane. Coenzyme Q (Ubiquinone) is the only non-protein component—it is a fat-soluble quinone.

3. Formation of ATP

As electrons flow from components of more negative redox potential to those with a more positive redox potential, energy is released. This beautiful flow is coupled to the synthesis of ATP by an enzyme called F0F1 ATPase.

There are three specific sites along the chain that provide enough energy to phosphorylate ADP into ATP:

  1. Oxidation of FMNH2 by Coenzyme Q.
  2. Oxidation of Cytochrome b by Cytochrome c1.
  3. Cytochrome oxidase reaction (Oxidation of Cyt a by Cyt a3).

Because NADH enters at Complex I, it passes through all 3 sites, yielding 3 ATPs. FADH2 enters at Complex II, bypassing the first site, thus yielding only 2 ATPs.

4. Inhibitors & Uncouplers

Substances that disrupt this delicate chain can be divided into three romantic tragedies: inhibitors of the chain, inhibitors of phosphorylation, and uncouplers.

Inhibitors of ETC Proper

  • Complex I: Barbiturates (Amobarbital), Piericidin A, Rotenone (insecticide).
  • Complex III: Antimycin A, Dimercaprol, BAL (British antilewisite).
  • Complex IV: Cyanide, Carbon Monoxide (CO), H2S. (Totally arrests respiration).

Inhibitors of Phosphorylation

These block the F0F1 ATPase directly.
Example: Oligomycin (an antibiotic).

Uncouplers & Ionophores

They allow electron transport but prevent ATP synthesis by destroying the proton (H+) gradient. Energy is released as heat instead of ATP.

  • Chemical: 2,4-Dinitrophenol (DNP), Dicoumarol, Salicylate (Aspirin metabolite).
  • Physiological: Thermogenin, Thyroxine, Bilirubin, Free fatty acids.
  • Ionophores: (Carry cations other than H+) Valinomycin, Gramicidin.

Quick Revision Notes

ETC at a Glance

Located in the inner mitochondrial membrane. Oxidizes NADH & FADH2 to generate ATP via oxidative phosphorylation. Ultimate electron acceptor is O2 (forms water).
