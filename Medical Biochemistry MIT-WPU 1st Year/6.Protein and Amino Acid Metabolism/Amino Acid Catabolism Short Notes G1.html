Amino Acid Catabolism

Comprehensive study notes and quick revision material for your exams.

Detailed Explanations

1. Introduction & Overview

  • • Energy Contribution: In adults, only 20% of amino acids from protein degradation are catabolized for energy (contributing 5-10% of total body energy). The remaining 80% are recycled for protein biosynthesis.
  • • No Storage: Unlike fats or carbohydrates, amino acids cannot be stored. Any excess protein consumed is immediately catabolized for energy.
  • • Increased Catabolism: Catabolism rates surge during starvation, uncontrolled diabetes, or when on a high-protein diet.
  • • Other Uses: Amino acids also act as precursors for creatine, hormones, glutathione, purines, and pyrimidines.

2. The Two Main Stages of Catabolism

The degradation of amino acids universally follows a two-stage process:

Stage 1: Amino Group Removal

The α-amino group is removed to form ammonia ($NH_3$). Because ammonia is toxic, the body rapidly converts it into urea, which is safely excreted via urine.

Stage 2: Carbon Skeleton Fate

The remaining α-keto acids (carbon skeletons) are transformed into TCA cycle intermediates or Acetyl-CoA. They are then oxidized for energy, or used to synthesize glucose/ketone bodies.

3. Deamination (Removal of Amino Groups)

A. Transamination

The transfer of an α-amino group from an amino acid to an α-keto acid acceptor (usually α-ketoglutarate).

  • Enzymes: Transaminases (Aminotransferases), located in mitochondria and cytosol.
  • Cofactor: Pyridoxal phosphate (PLP).
  • Goal: Collects amino groups into Glutamate.
  • Example: Aspartate + α-Ketoglutarate ⇌ Oxaloacetate + Glutamate (Catalyzed by Aspartate Amino Transferase).

B. Oxidative Deamination

The liberation of the collected amino groups as free ammonia.

  • Primary Mechanism: Glutamate Dehydrogenase acts on Glutamate to produce α-ketoglutarate and Ammonia ($NH_3$). Requires $NAD^+$ or $NADP^+$.
  • Regulation: ATP and GTP are allosteric inhibitors; ADP and GDP are activators.
  • Direct Oxidases: D-amino acid oxidase (FAD-dependent, acts on glycine) and L-amino acid oxidase (FMN-dependent). They convert amino acids to imino acids, followed by hydrolytic loss of ammonia.

C. Non-Oxidative Deamination

Specific enzymes remove ammonia without oxidation.

  • Serine Dehydratase: Converts Serine → Pyruvate + $NH_3$ + $H_2O$. (Requires PLP).
  • Cysteine Desulfhydrase: Converts Cysteine → Pyruvate + $NH_3$ + $H_2S$. (Requires PLP, found in bacteria).
  • Threonine Dehydratase: Acts on threonine.

4. Decarboxylation

The removal of a $CO_2$ group from the carboxyl group of an amino acid, resulting in the formation of physiologically significant biogenic amines.

  • Enzymes: Amino acid decarboxylases.
  • Cofactor: Pyridoxal phosphate (PLP).
  • Locations: Liver, spleen, kidney, intestine, lung, and brain.
Important Examples:
  • Histidine → Histamine
  • Tryptophan → Tryptamine
  • Tyrosine → Tyramine

Short Notes (Exam Revision)

Basic Facts

  • • Only 20% of degraded AAs used for energy.
  • • AAs cannot be stored; excess = energy.
  • • Catabolism ↑ in starvation, diabetes, high protein diet.

The Two Stages

  • 1. Deamination: Amino group removed → Ammonia ($NH_3$) → Urea (urine).
  • 2. Carbon Skeleton: Converted to TCA intermediates / Acetyl-CoA → Energy / Glucose / Ketones.

Transamination

  • • Transfer of $NH_2$ to α-ketoglutarate.
  • • Forms Glutamate (collecting point).
  • • Enzyme: Transaminases.
  • • Cofactor required: Pyridoxal phosphate (PLP).

Oxidative Deamination

  • • Glutamate Dehydrogenase: Glutamate → α-ketoglutarate + $NH_3$.
  • • Requires $NAD^+$ / $NADP^+$.
  • • Regulators: Inhibited by ATP/GTP. Activated by ADP/GDP.

Non-Oxidative Deamination

  • • Direct removal of $NH_3$.
  • • Serine dehydratase: Serine → Pyruvate.
  • • Cysteine desulfhydrase: Cysteine → Pyruvate + $H_2S$.

Decarboxylation

  • • Removal of $CO_2$ to form biogenic amines.
  • • Cofactor: PLP.
  • • Histidine → Histamine
  • • Tryptophan → Tryptamine
  • • Tyrosine → Tyramine
