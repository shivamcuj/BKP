Introduction to Mutations

The genetic macromolecule DNA is highly stable, but it is not entirely exempt from gradual changes. A mutation refers to a permanent change in the DNA structure of a gene.

Mutagens are the substances or chemicals that induce these changes. The alterations in DNA are subsequently reflected during cellular processes like replication, transcription, and translation. If mutations occur in somatic cells, they may lead to cancer, whereas mutations in germ cells are inherited by the progeny.

Types of Mutations

1. Point Mutations (Base Substitution)

Occurs when a single base pair in the DNA is replaced by another. They are classified into two subtypes:

  • Transitions: A purine is replaced by another purine (A ↔ G), or a pyrimidine is replaced by another pyrimidine (C ↔ T).
  • Transversions: A purine is replaced by a pyrimidine, or vice versa (e.g., A ↔ C or G ↔ T).

Consequences of Point Mutations:

A. Silent Mutation

The altered mRNA codon still codes for the same amino acid due to the degeneracy of the genetic code. There is no detectable effect on the protein.

Example: UCA (Serine) changes to UCU (still Serine).

B. Missense Mutation

The altered base codes for a different amino acid. The severity depends on where it occurs and what the new amino acid is.

  • Acceptable: The protein still functions normally. (e.g., Hb Hikari: Asparagine substitutes Lysine at position 61 of the β-globin chain).
  • Partially Acceptable: The protein has compromised function. (e.g., HbS / Sickle-cell anemia: Glutamic acid is replaced by Valine at position 6 of the β-chain).
  • Unacceptable: The protein becomes nonfunctional. (e.g., HbM / Methemoglobin: Histidine is replaced by Tyrosine, making Hb unable to transport oxygen).
C. Nonsense Mutation

The altered codon becomes a stop/termination signal (UAA, UAG, UGA), causing premature termination of protein synthesis. The resulting protein is usually nonfunctional.

Example: Certain types of Thalassemia (e.g., UGG Tryptophan → UGA Stop codon).

2. Frameshift Mutations

Occurs when one or more base pairs are inserted into or deleted from the DNA sequence. Because the genetic code is read in triplets without punctuation, adding or removing nucleotides (not in multiples of 3) shifts the entire reading frame.

Deletion

A base is removed, altering the reading frame from that point onward.

Example: Cystic Fibrosis (often caused by deletion).

Insertion

A new base is added, pushing the rest of the sequence out of frame.

Example: Certain forms of Thalassemia.

Note: If nucleotides are inserted/deleted in multiples of 3, a frameshift does not occur; instead, an entire amino acid is added or missing, which is generally less severe.

DNA Repair Mechanisms

Cells possess innate systems to repair damaged DNA, preventing serious consequences. There are four major mechanisms:

1

Base Excision-Repair

Fixes: Damage to a single base (e.g., spontaneous depurination where cytosine turns to uracil).

Mechanism: Uracil DNA glycosylase removes the defective base. An Endonuclease cuts the backbone, removing a few bases. DNA polymerase and DNA ligase fill and seal the gap.

2

Nucleotide Excision-Repair

Fixes: Large-scale defects in a DNA segment caused by UV light, radiation, or chemicals (e.g., cross-linkages).

Mechanism: DNA is unwound. An Exinuclease (excision nuclease) cuts the DNA on both sides of the defect. The segment is removed and replaced by DNA polymerase and ligase.

Clinical Note: Defects here cause Xeroderma pigmentosum (XP), making patients highly sensitive to UV light and skin cancers.
3

Mismatch Repair

Fixes: Copying errors during replication (e.g., matching C with A instead of T with A).

Mechanism: The system identifies the new, incorrect strand because it is not yet methylated (unlike the template). GATC endonuclease cuts the unmethylated strand, Exonuclease removes the defect, and new DNA is synthesized and ligated.

Clinical Note: Defects here are linked to Hereditary nonpolyposis colon cancer (HNPCC).
4

Double-Strand Break Repair

Fixes: Severe damage from ionizing radiation, free radicals, or chemotherapy.

Mechanism: Requires unwinding, alignment, and ligation. Repaired via homologous recombination (common in yeast) or non-homologous end joining (dominant in mammals).

Flash Notes: Mutations

Core Concepts

  • Mutation: Permanent DNA change.
  • Mutagen: Chemical/factor inducing mutation.
  • Somatic vs Germ: Somatic = cancer risk; Germ = inherited diseases.

Point Mutations (Base Sub.)

  • Transition: Purine↔Purine or Pyr↔Pyr.
  • Transversion: Purine↔Pyrimidine.

Consequences of Point Mutations

Silent

Same amino acid. No effect.

Missense

Different amino acid.
• Acceptable: Hb Hikari
• Partial: HbS (Sickle cell)
• Unacceptable: HbM

Nonsense

Creates STOP codon. Premature termination (e.g., Thalassemia).

Frameshift Mutations

  • Insertion or Deletion of 1 or 2 bases. Alters entire reading frame.
  • Deletion Ex: Cystic Fibrosis.
  • Insertion Ex: Thalassemia.
  • Note: Insert/Delete of 3 bases = no frameshift, just missing/extra amino acid.

Flash Notes: DNA Repair

Base Excision

Fixes single base (e.g., U instead of C).
Key Enzyme: Uracil DNA Glycosylase.

Nucleotide Excision

Fixes large defects (UV damage).
Disease link: Xeroderma pigmentosum (XP).

Mismatch Repair

Fixes replication errors. Targets unmethylated new strand.
Disease link: HNPCC (Colon cancer).

Double-Strand Break

Fixes major radiation damage. Uses non-homologous end joining (mammals) or homologous recombination.
